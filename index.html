Why we built it

A deaf or speech-impaired person can sign fluently and still not be understood, because the person across from them never learned to sign. That happens at a hospital reception, in a classroom, at a government office — ordinary places, every day.

Interpreters are not always available and not always wanted. The gap is usually small: a handful of words, said quickly, to someone who only needs to understand this once. We wanted to close that gap with something a person can simply wear.

Why it matters in healthcare

Outside a hospital, a missed word is inconvenient. Inside one, it changes what care a person receives.

  • Improves communication between deaf/mute patients and healthcare providers
  • Reduces misunderstandings during medical consultations
  • Enables faster and more accurate communication in emergencies
  • Increases patient independence and confidence
  • Helps healthcare staff understand patients without always needing a sign language interpreter
  • Supports better patient care and treatment decisions
  • Provides a portable, low-cost assistive technology solution

This prototype recognizes a small vocabulary and is not a medical device. It shows an approach — a wearable that speaks for the patient — rather than a finished clinical tool.

How it works

The signal starts as a bend in a finger and ends as a word in two places at once.

  1. Hand

    The gesture

    Fingers bend. The wrist turns. Both carry meaning.

  2. Sense

    Five flex sensors + MPU9250

    Each flex sensor changes resistance as the finger curls. The MPU9250 reports how the hand is tilted and how it is moving.

  3. Decide

    Arduino Nano

    Every sensor value is checked against a calibrated range. When all of them fall inside the range for one gesture, that gesture is recognized.

  4. Output

    Two channels at once

    Seen — HM-10 Bluetooth Low Energy sends the text to a smartphone.

    Heard — a DFPlayer Mini plays the matching audio file through a speaker.

Two modes, no buttons

The glove switches between modes with a gesture of its own, so the hand never has to leave the conversation to press something. A further gesture clears the text.

The prototype

Future improvements

  • Support for all 26 letters of the ASL (American Sign Language) alphabet
  • An AI-supported version that learns each wearer instead of using fixed thresholds
  • A left-hand version of the glove
  • Support for more spoken languages
  • Support for local sign languages, such as Kurdish and Arabic sign language
  • A custom PCB with smaller sensors, so the electronics sit flatter and lighter on the arm
  • A dedicated mobile app in place of a general Bluetooth terminal
